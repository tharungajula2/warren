Credit risk has two fundamentally different loss layers.

**Expected loss (EL)** is the probability-weighted average loss generated by ordinary credit activity under the specified horizon and parameter definitions. At exposure level, the familiar decomposition is PD × LGD × EAD. At portfolio level, expected losses aggregate across exposures, subject to coherent measurement.

Because EL is expected, it belongs in the economics of doing business. Lending that earns a gross spread of 3% but carries 2% expected credit loss does not have 3% available to pay operating cost, funding cost, tax and return on capital. Expected loss is a cost of taking credit risk even when no particular loan is certain to default.

**Unexpected loss (UL)** is the variability beyond that expected amount. It is driven not only by individual PD and LGD but by concentration, correlation, parameter uncertainty and adverse systematic states. It is this tail that can overwhelm current earnings and ordinary provisions.

Capital therefore sits behind the expected-loss layer. Basel's IRB architecture explicitly designs risk-weight functions around unexpected loss and treats expected loss separately through its expected-loss and provision framework.

The separation is conceptual rather than a claim that every accounting or regulatory regime draws exactly the same numerical boundary. Accounting allowances, prudential provisions, regulatory capital and internal economic capital follow different rule books. In particular, Indian scheduled commercial banks remain under RBI prudential provisioning rather than Ind AS 109 because RBI deferred bank Ind AS implementation until further notice. Relevant NBFCs applying Ind AS are a separate population.

The complete architecture is therefore:

<Flow>
+------------------------+
| Credit exposure        |
+------------------------+
            |
            v
+------------------------+
| PD x LGD x EAD         |
| expected loss          |
+------------------------+
            |
            v
+------------------------+
| Pricing / provisions / |
| ordinary loss capacity |
+------------------------+

+------------------------+
| Loss distribution tail |
| concentration +        |
| correlation + stress   |
+------------------------+
            |
            v
+------------------------+
| Unexpected loss        |
+------------------------+
            |
            v
+------------------------+
| Capital                |
| solvency protection    |
+------------------------+
</Flow>

A profitable credit business therefore requires more than low defaults. Pricing must compensate for expected loss and operating/funding costs while generating an adequate return for the capital committed against unexpected loss.

<Rule label="PERMANENT RULE">
Expected loss is a cost to be anticipated; unexpected loss is a solvency risk to be capitalised.
</Rule>

<Trap label="PERMANENT TRAP">
Treating provisions and capital as two interchangeable pots of "money for bad loans" destroys the distinction between an expected expense and protection against the tail of the loss distribution.
</Trap>

<NeverConfuse>
**Provision or expected-loss capacity** — recognition or absorption of credit losses expected under the applicable accounting or prudential framework.

**Capital** — loss-absorbing resources protecting solvency against adverse outcomes beyond ordinary expected loss, subject to the applicable capital regime.
</NeverConfuse>

<Rule label="NO DELTA">
**No delta.** The economic distinction between expected and unexpected loss is universal. Accounting provision rules and regulatory capital calculations differ materially across jurisdictions, so the conceptual split must not be mistaken for identical accounting treatment.
</Rule>

<Recall
  q="Why should expected loss enter loan pricing?"
  a="Because it is a recurring economic cost of extending credit, even though the particular borrowers that will default are uncertain."
