After estimating a parameter, say the default rate of a loan grade, we compute a CI. A 95% CI from 1.8% to 2.4% means if we repeated the borrower selection many times, about 95% of those intervals would include the true default rate. Importantly, it doesn’t mean “95% chance the true rate lies in this specific interval.” The true rate is fixed (unknown); the CI’s endpoints are random from sample variance.

In credit risk, CIs are crucial. For example, IRB models often use confidence bounds on PDs or LGDs to ensure conservatism. When validating a new PD model, a regulator might require that observed default rates fall within the model’s forecast CI. If 2% forecast with ±0.2% margin, and observed is 2.3%, the interval check helps assess if deviation is within sampling noise or indicates miscalibration.

**Calculation:** For large samples, a CI can be approximate normal: estimate ± z*SE, where SE is the standard error of the estimate. For proportion PD, SE = sqrt(p*(1-p)/n). For small samples or rare events, use exact (binomial) or bootstrap CIs. Also, always report confidence level (e.g. 95%) and sample size context.

In summary, CI expresses estimation uncertainty: wider if data are sparse or variable, narrower with more/larger data. The permanent rule: always pair point estimates (like PD) with a CI to show reliability. Regulators often look at these intervals when backtesting models.

```text
Sample PD = 2.0%  (50 defaults/2500 loans)
SE = sqrt(0.02*0.98/2500) ≈ 0.009
95% CI ≈ 2.0% ± 1.96*0.9% = [0.22%, 3.78%]
```

### Permanent rule

Report estimates with their confidence intervals. The interval indicates how much estimation error might exist due to finite data.

### Permanent trap

Misinterpreting CI as probability of parameter. E.g. “We’re 95% sure true PD is between 1.8–2.4%” is wrong. Actually, if we repeated data collection, 95% of such intervals would contain the true PD.

### Never confuse

**95% confidence interval** — range capturing the true parameter in 95% of repeated samples.

---

**Credible interval (Bayesian)** — range where the parameter has 95% probability given the data (Bayesian concept). CI’s guarantee is about the method, not the single interval’s probability.

### Jurisdiction variants

**No delta.** Statistical confidence intervals are universal. Financial regulations (e.g. RBI or ECB validation) may stipulate certain confidence levels, but methodology is the same.
