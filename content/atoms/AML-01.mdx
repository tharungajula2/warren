Money laundering is the process of making illegally-gained money appear legal.  In common terms, it involves introducing criminal proceeds into the financial system (placement), moving them through complex transactions to obscure origins (layering), and finally using them in legitimate markets (integration).  For example, a drug trafficker may deposit cash into a front-company’s bank account (placement), rapidly move funds between accounts or countries (layering), and then use the cleansed funds to buy real estate (integration).  The FATF emphasizes that authorities should consider money laundering risks at all stages – placement, layering and integration – even though in reality these can overlap.

Placement – physically putting illicit cash into the financial system (e.g. cash deposits, buying chips at a casino) – is very risky for criminals.  Layering – transferring funds through multiple accounts, instruments or entities to hide trails – creates complexity but can arouse bank suspicions through unusual patterns.  Integration – investing laundered money into legitimate businesses or assets (real estate, luxury goods) – lets criminals enjoy their proceeds.  Importantly, real-world laundering seldom follows a neat one-way flow; criminals may cycle funds or intermingle legitimate and illicit transactions to muddy the trail.

A bank’s AML program must detect signs of these stages. For instance, sudden large cash deposits by a new customer (suggesting placement) or a string of rapid transfers through offshore accounts (suggesting layering) should trigger scrutiny.  Because money laundering is defined by its intent rather than just specific steps, banks use broad Suspicious Transaction Report (STR) criteria – any transaction “indicating proceeds of crime” – rather than looking only for textbook P-L-I patterns.

<Rule label="PERMANENT RULE">
Banks must monitor for unusual deposits, rapid transfers, and sudden asset purchases by new or high-risk customers, as these may indicate placement, layering or integration of illicit funds.
</Rule>

<Trap label="PERMANENT TRAP">
Assuming all money laundering neatly follows “placement → layering → integration” can blind analysts to schemes where funds re-enter or loop through the system in non-linear ways.
</Trap>

<NeverConfuse a="Money laundering" b="Proceeds-based financing (e.g. fraud)">
**Money laundering** — processing criminal proceeds to hide their origin.
---
**Proceeds-based financing (e.g. fraud)** — using illicit or legitimate funds to finance any activity. The confusion arises when a loan might be secured with dirty money; laundering focuses on hiding sources, whereas the loan per se is a financial crime enabled by those hidden funds.
</NeverConfuse>

<Rule label="NO DELTA">
**No delta.** The core concept of money laundering (concealing criminal funds) is universal, though each country’s law (e.g. India’s PMLA, US BSA, UK POCA) defines specific offences.
</Rule>
