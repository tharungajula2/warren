Banks historically maintained data in silos and spreadsheets, impeding a holistic risk view. When Lehman Brothers collapsed in 2008, many institutions realized their systems could not tell the board or regulators the full picture of their exposures. The Basel Committee responded with BCBS 239 (2013) to “strengthen banks’ risk data aggregation capabilities and internal risk reporting practices”. Globally systemically important banks (G-SIBs) had to comply by 2016; supervisors applied similar standards to domestic SIBs. In practice, risk data discipline means implementing corporate-wide data governance, architecture and controls so that any material risk metric can be produced “fully, quickly and accurately.” This underpins effective risk management and regulatory compliance.

Key outcomes of this discipline include board-level data governance, documented processes, and accountability for data quality. It also requires banks to invest in integrated data architectures and automation, since manual consolidation is deemed unacceptable. The discipline encompasses ongoing improvement: recent Basel progress reports note that, years on, many banks still need work to fully meet BCBS 239.

### Permanent rule

Timely, accurate risk data is essential for sound decision-making and regulatory compliance.

### Permanent trap

Relying on last-minute, manual spreadsheet aggregations (especially during stress) can give a false sense of security.

### Never confuse

**Risk data** — Information on potential losses or exposures (e.g. credit, market risk metrics). It is forward-looking and used for risk management.

---

**Accounting (financial) data** — Realized financial transactions (P&amp;L, balance sheets) as recorded in accounts. Risk data and accounting data serve different purposes and must be reconciled, not conflated.

### Jurisdiction variants

**No delta.** The need for robust risk data aggregation and reporting is a universal lesson from the financial crisis.
