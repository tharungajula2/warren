In a derivative trade, the credit exposure at any time is the replacement cost (current market value if positive) plus any expected future exposure. The exposure is never equal to the notional amount of the contract; instead it varies with underlying prices. Unlike a one-sided loan, both parties in a derivative trade can be in-the-money or out-of-the-money.  If one party defaults, the surviving party may suffer loss equal to the mark‑to‑market value of the contract. CCR therefore depends on both counterparty credit quality and the volatility of the derivative’s value.

Banks typically net exposures under enforceable master agreements and may require collateral. Managing CCR requires measuring current exposure and forecasting potential future exposure. Basel guidelines define metrics like expected exposure to capture these dynamics. CCR contrasts with normal credit risk: a loan’s exposure is usually the principal, while a derivative’s exposure is uncertain and can reverse direction as market prices change.

<Rule label="PERMANENT RULE">
Counterparty credit exposures must account for current mark-to-market values and any future potential changes – not just notional amounts.
</Rule>

<Trap label="PERMANENT TRAP">
Assuming a derivative’s credit exposure equals its notional or treating CCR as a one-sided loan risk. In reality, exposure fluctuates and is bilateral.
</Trap>

<NeverConfuse a="Credit risk (loans)" b="Counterparty credit risk (derivatives)">
**Credit risk (loans)** — Risk of borrower default on funded loan balance, usually fixed (principal).
---
**Counterparty credit risk (derivatives)** — Risk of counterparty default on contingent obligations; exposure is mark-to-market and can be positive or negative for either party.
</NeverConfuse>

<Rule label="NO DELTA">
**No delta.** The basic concept of bilateral, contingent exposure applies universally.
</Rule>
