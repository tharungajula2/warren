In a securities-lending transaction, the lender transfers securities and the borrower undertakes to return equivalent securities later. The borrower provides collateral, commonly cash or other securities, and pays an economic fee. If cash collateral is used, the lender may invest it and rebate part of the return; if non-cash collateral is used, the fee is usually stated more directly.

The borrower needs the security itself rather than simply cash. Common motives include covering a short sale, avoiding a settlement failure, supporting market-making inventory or facilitating arbitrage. A security that is difficult to borrow can command a high fee because its scarcity has economic value.

Legal title and economic exposure must be separated. Under title-transfer forms, the borrower becomes legal owner of the delivered securities and can use them, but owes equivalent securities back. The lender generally retains the economic benefit of distributions through manufactured payments or contractual adjustments. Corporate actions therefore require careful processing: dividend, voting, rights and recall mechanics can affect the lender differently from outright ownership.

Collateralisation reduces counterparty exposure but creates operational and market risks. Collateral must be valued, haircuts applied, margin calls processed and concentration limits monitored. If collateral is reinvested, the lender also takes investment and liquidity risk in the reinvestment portfolio.

The Global Master Securities Lending Agreement, or GMSLA, published by ISLA, is a major market-standard agreement for securities lending. Different versions use different collateral structures, including title-transfer and security-interest approaches. Documentation determines default, close-out and collateral rights; the product label alone does not.

The closest economic cousin is repo, but the transaction driver differs: repo usually starts with a need for cash financing; securities lending usually starts with a need for a specific security.

<Rule label="PERMANENT RULE">
Ask what the scarce asset is: in securities lending it is usually the security; in repo it is usually funding, unless the repo is special.
</Rule>

<Trap label="PERMANENT TRAP">
Calling a securities loan an unsecured loan because the borrower can use the security. Collateral and master-agreement mechanics are central.
</Trap>

<NeverConfuse a="Securities lending" b="Repo">
