When a bank owes a counterparty, its deteriorating credit actually reduces the expected payment, hence an accounting gain (DVA). However, regulators typically do not allow banks to rely on DVA gains to reduce capital or to distribute to shareholders. DVA mainly matters for pricing certain trades. Some regulators net CVA and DVA differently for capital.

Funding Valuation Adjustment (FVA) is another concept: it accounts for the cost of funding collateral. If a bank must borrow to post collateral, that funding cost reduces trade value (FVA). FVA debates arose around whether to include it in fair value; many banks include FVA/MVA (margin valuation adjustment) in their pricing for completeness.

### Permanent rule

Include DVA in valuations if following mid-market accounting, but regulatory capital ignores DVA (no capital relief). FVA should be considered in pricing, but typical regulatory capital frameworks do not have a specific FVA charge (it is considered in IRRBB, not CCR capital).

### Permanent trap

Double-counting or incorrect offset of CVA by DVA. While both arise from same framework, regulations treat them asymmetrically (CVA is capitalized, DVA is not).

### Never confuse

**CVA** (benefit from widening credit spreads of the counterparty) vs **DVA** (benefit from widening credit spreads of yourself). They cancel in a risk-neutral value if both are allowed, but only CVA is capital-adjusted.

### Jurisdiction variants

**No delta.** These valuation adjustments are accounting concepts; regulatory treatment is broadly similar: CVA capital is required, DVA yields no capital benefit.
