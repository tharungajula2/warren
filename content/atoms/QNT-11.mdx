### Core mechanism

| Structure | Credit example | Statistical consequence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Positive correlation | Higher utilization and higher arrears tendency | Variables move together |
| Negative correlation | Higher score and lower observed default rate | Variables move oppositely |
| Common-driver dependence | Unemployment shock affects many borrowers | Loan outcomes become correlated |
| Confounding | Income relates to both loan size and default | Apparent loan-size effect may change after controlling for income |
| Proxy relationship | Region carries information about local economy | Predictive association need not be causal |

```text
Macro shock ↑ → borrower stress ↑ → defaults ↑
Macro shock ↑ → utilization ↑
Observed utilization and default therefore move together
```

Correlation can be useful for prediction even without causality, but causal language requires stronger design and assumptions. In credit models, the practical danger is to interpret a stable predictive association as a mechanism that will remain unchanged after policy, portfolio or economic conditions move.

### Where it sits

```text
Upstream:   Lending variables · Event outcomes · Common macro drivers

```

### Formula

```text
Correlation = covariance of X and Y / (standard deviation of X x standard deviation of Y)
```

### Permanent rule

Prediction can use association; causal interpretation cannot be obtained from correlation alone.

### Never confuse [Correlation] and [Causation]

**Correlation** - statistical co-movement between variables.

---

**Causation** - changing one factor produces a change in another under a defensible causal structure.

### Permanent trap

Reading a regression or correlation coefficient as a policy lever. A variable may be predictive because it proxies another condition, so changing the variable itself may not change credit risk.
