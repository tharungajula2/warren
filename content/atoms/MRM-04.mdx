In practice, there is a boundary between quantitative models and simpler tools.  U.S. SR 26-2 says it excludes “simple arithmetic calculations, such as those found within spreadsheets, as well as deterministic rule-based processes” from the definition of a model.  This means a spreadsheet that just adds up values or applies fixed formulas (with no statistical algorithm) is not a “model” under Fed/OCC guidance.  By contrast, the UK PRA says that even deterministic methods may need controls if they are complex and impact decisions.  RBI’s draft guidance goes further: it says any tool (including decision rules in software) that has a material impact on decision-making should be treated as a model.  (For example, a loan-pricing spreadsheet might be just a “basic tool,” but if it factors in risk-adjusted spreads to set rates, RBI says it must be treated as a model.)

The key distinction is whether the method relies on a statistical/economic theory or not.  **If there is no statistical model underlying it, it is formally a “tool,”** but regulators still expect robust controls.  The PRA advises firms to document and control deterministic rules just as they would a model.  In short, ***deterministic tools*** are fixed-logic processes (e.g. credit-policy rules coded into a system) whereas ***statistical models*** use probabilistic or econometric methods.  Firms should not ignore the risk of a rule-based tool just because it’s not labeled a model.  In many cases, best practice is to apply parts of the model risk framework (validation, review) to critical tools.

### Permanent rule

Tools with complex business impact should be governed even if not classified as “models.”  Always clarify in policy which calculations are covered by MRM.

### Permanent trap

Assuming “not a model = no risk.”  For example, ignoring validation of a spreadsheet pricing tool because it’s deemed a “non-model.”  (In reality, a faulty spreadsheet can cause major errors.)

### Never confuse

**Deterministic tool** — a fixed‐logic calculator or rules engine (no statistical basis).  It may be excluded from the model definition but still needs oversight.
---
**Statistical model** — uses statistical/economic theory and assumptions to produce predictions (subject to full MRM). They differ: one is theory-driven (model), the other is rule-based (tool).

### Jurisdiction variants

**IN** — RBI’s draft treats even deterministic tools as models if they impact decisions.  E.g., a spreadsheet deciding loan rates is a model if it drives pricing.
**UK** — PRA excludes simple calculators from “model” definition but says complex decision rules may still be subject to MRM controls.
**US** — SR 26-2 explicitly **excludes** basic spreadsheets or rule-based software from the model definition, although sound governance requires controls on high-impact tools.
**EU** — Like the UK, EU supervisors focus on any business-critical method; a simple tool might not be called a model, but it must be validated if it functions like one. (ECB’s internal-model guide treats any algorithm with “material impact” similarly.)
