Credit instruments are often analysed relative to a lower-risk benchmark rather than by absolute yield alone. A simple spread may be the yield on a corporate bond minus the yield on a government bond of similar maturity. More sophisticated measures include interpolated spreads, asset-swap spreads and option-adjusted spreads. Each answers a slightly different question.

A wider spread means the market demands more compensation relative to the reference. Spreads may widen because the issuer’s perceived default risk rises, recovery expectations fall, market liquidity deteriorates, investors reduce risk appetite, supply increases or the benchmark itself moves differently. Conversely, improving credit quality or strong demand may tighten spreads.

Spread is not expected loss. In a stylised credit model, expected loss depends on probability of default, loss given default and exposure. A market credit spread includes more than that expected loss: investors may require compensation for uncertainty, downgrade risk, liquidity, capital usage and market imbalances. This is why reverse-engineering a probability of default from a raw spread without a model can be misleading.

Spread risk also matters before any default occurs. If a bond’s spread widens, its market value usually falls even if the issuer continues paying. The price sensitivity to spread moves is often measured with spread duration or credit spread DV01 rather than pure interest-rate duration.

Reference choice matters. A spread to a sovereign curve, swap curve or matched government security can differ. Comparing two spread series without checking the benchmark, maturity mapping, option treatment and price source can create false conclusions.

For bank trading and risk data, spread fields should therefore be treated as calculated measures with lineage: underlying price, reference curve, interpolation rule, optionality treatment and timestamp all matter.

```text
Bond yield
   |
   +--&gt; reference-rate component
   |
   +--&gt; credit / liquidity / risk premium
               |
               v
          credit spread
```

### Permanent rule

A credit spread is a relative market price of risk, not a pure measure of default probability.

### Permanent trap

Reading a 300 bp spread as a 3% annual probability of default without specifying recovery, horizon and pricing model.

### Never confuse

**Credit spread** — yield or pricing difference relative to a chosen reference.

---

**Expected credit loss** — modelled loss from default risk, commonly driven by PD, LGD and exposure.
