Current exposure is simply the contract’s current market value (if it is positive to the bank). For example, if a bank has an interest rate swap that is in-the-money by $1 million, its current exposure is $1 million. Potential future exposure (PFE) represents a statistically high percentile of the future distribution of exposures. It accounts for volatility: even if current exposure is small, markets could move. PFE is computed using models or add-on factors to the notional. Together, current and potential exposures are used to calculate Expected Exposure (EE) and Expected Positive Exposure (EPE) profiles over the life of the contract.

An exposure profile shows how exposure might evolve. Initially, current exposure starts at the trade date’s mark‑to‑market. Over time, without frequent cash settlement (as in uncollateralized trades), exposure can grow. Banks use either simulation or standardized add-ons to gauge PFE at future dates. Current and potential exposures are summed and multiplied by a buffer (alpha) in SA-CCR to get Exposure At Default (EAD).

```text
Current Exposure (CE) = max(mark-to-market, 0)
Potential Future Exposure (PFE) = high-percentile of future exposure increase
EAD = 1.4 × (CE + PFE)   (under SA-CCR)
```

### Permanent rule

When measuring CCR, always include both current exposure (today’s value) and potential future exposure (possible increase).

### Permanent trap

Considering only current exposure (ignoring future volatility) will understate CCR. Conversely, overstating exposure by using notional without adjustments is also wrong.

### Never confuse

**Current Exposure** — Today’s positive mark-to-market value.
---
**Potential Future Exposure (PFE)** — A modeled high-percentile future exposure. PFE is *not* the same as current exposure or an average; it is an estimate of future increase and is typically larger.

### Jurisdiction variants

**No delta.** The concepts of current vs potential exposure are part of the Basel CCR framework and apply universally.
