Each requirement (or user story) needs acceptance criteria that describe exactly what “done” means.  They are written from the user’s perspective: e.g. “The search function returns results that match 100% or partially matching the query” (as in one example). According to Atlassian, acceptance criteria are “clear, concise, and testable statements” that define what success looks like.  They should be measurable: instead of vague terms (“fast”, “user-friendly”), use objective thresholds (e.g. “90% of transactions processed within 2 seconds”). Each criterion should map to one or more executable tests.

Good acceptance criteria eliminate ambiguity.  They align development and QA teams and allow stakeholders to verify requirements. As one guide puts it, they define *“what success looks like”* and “translate the purpose into explicit, verifiable requirements”. For regulators, acceptance criteria often double as test cases to demonstrate compliance.

### Permanent rule

Write acceptance criteria that are specific and measurable. Each criterion must be objectively verifiable (e.g. through a test or demo). If you can’t define a pass/fail condition, refine the requirement.

### Permanent trap

Vague criteria. For example, “The system must be user-friendly” or “Reports should load quickly” are not testable. Also avoid combining multiple conditions into one criterion. Each criterion should stand alone (independent).

### Never confuse

**Acceptance Criteria** vs **Test Case**:
*Acceptance Criteria* are the high-level conditions to meet for a requirement to be accepted. A *Test Case* is a detailed script with steps to verify those criteria. They serve related purposes (criteria shape tests) but are not the same thing.

### No delta

The practice of defining testable acceptance criteria is universal across methodologies and jurisdictions.
