Credit-risk measurement starts with an event definition. A model cannot estimate a **probability of default** until "default" has been specified. Changing the definition changes the target variable even when the borrowers and their behaviour remain identical.

Basel's regulatory architecture uses two main routes for obligor default: the bank considers the obligor **unlikely to pay** its credit obligations in full without recourse to actions such as realising security, or a material credit obligation is more than 90 days past due. The past-due test is therefore one route into default, not the complete meaning of default.

The level of application also matters. Wholesale default is generally obligor-centred: serious failure by the obligor may affect the treatment of multiple obligations. Retail frameworks can permit facility-level application under prescribed conditions because mass retail portfolios are often managed at the individual-product or facility level.

Default is also distinct from **bankruptcy**. Legal insolvency may be strong evidence that an obligor is unlikely to pay, but regulatory default can arise before a formal insolvency proceeding. Conversely, default is not the same as realised loss: an exposure may default and subsequently recover substantially or even completely.

Consistency is essential through the measurement chain. Historical default rates, PD calibration, LGD workout samples, rating migration and validation must use compatible definitions. Mixing a 90-day model-development target with a different production default rule produces apparently precise but conceptually invalid statistics.

<Rule label="PERMANENT RULE">
Define default before measuring default risk, and preserve that definition across development, calibration, validation and reporting.
</Rule>

<Trap label="PERMANENT TRAP">
Historical "bad" flags are often treated as interchangeable even though one source means 90 days past due, another means charge-off, and another means bankruptcy.
</Trap>

<NeverConfuse>
**Default** — a specified credit-risk state triggered by defined criteria.

**Loss** — the economic or accounting shortfall ultimately suffered; default can occur without a full loss.
</NeverConfuse>

<Delta j="IN">
— RBI commercial-bank IRACP classification is centred on prudential NPA rules. A term loan becomes NPA when interest or principal remains overdue for more than 90 days, with separate rules for OD/CC, agricultural and other exposures. RBI's current directions separately cross-reference the regulatory meaning of “default” used in stressed-asset resolution. [RBI Commercial Banks IRACP Directions 2025]
</Delta>

<Delta j="UK">
— the current PRA framework implements the CRR-style definition of default and its materiality requirements; the PRA's replacement SS3/24 framework is due to apply from 1 January 2027. [PRA PS7/19; SS3/24]
</Delta>

<Delta j="EU">
— CRR Article 178 uses unlikeliness to pay and material past-due criteria, supported by EBA Guidelines on application of the definition of default. Those Guidelines were amended in May 2026. [CRR Article 178; EBA/GL/2016/07 as amended by EBA/GL/2026/05]
</Delta>

<Delta j="US">
— Advanced Approaches rules distinguish wholesale and retail default. Wholesale uses unlikeliness to pay or more than 90 days past due on a material obligation, while specified retail categories use different mechanical past-due thresholds. [12 CFR Part 217, Subpart E]
</Delta>

<Recall
  q="Why must default be defined before PD?"
  a="PD is the probability of a particular defined event; changing the event changes the parameter."
/>

<Recall
  q="Is 90 days past due the entire Basel definition of default?"
  a="No. It is one default trigger alongside unlikeliness to pay."
