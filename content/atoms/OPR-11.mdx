Basel II allowed a most sophisticated option, the **Advanced Measurement Approach (AMA)**, for large banks. Under AMA, each bank built internal models (often loss distribution approach) using its own loss data, scenarios, external data and BEICFs. They targeted a 99.9% VaR for operational loss. AMA banks required heavy internal modeling and sophisticated risk frameworks.

However, the financial crisis exposed problems with AMA.  In practice AMA outputs varied wildly between banks, making the capital numbers non-comparable.  The extreme tail of the loss distribution is highly sensitive to inputs: risk modelers found that a ~9% change in a severity parameter could double the capital charge.  This instability (and complexity/supervisory burden) led regulators to scrap AMA in Basel 3.1.  As a result, AMA ceased being an approved Pillar 1 method globally after 2021.

<Rule label="PERMANENT RULE">
When AMA was in use, regulators required it to be conservative. Now banks base Pillar 1 capital on SMA instead.  (Many banks still use AMA-like internal models for economic capital or Pillar 2 stress tests.)
</Rule>

<Trap label="PERMANENT TRAP">
Don’t rely on unvalidated internal models for regulatory capital.  Overfitting to your own data, or using only small-sample scenarios, gave illusion of precision and was a key flaw of AMA.
</Trap>

<NeverConfuse>
**AMA (old Basel)** vs **Standardised Measurement Approach (SMA)**.  AMA = internal models; SMA = formulaic Basel 3.1 approach. They are completely different regimes.
</NeverConfuse>

<Rule label="NO DELTA">
**No delta.** All Basel-member regulators moved away from AMA.  For example, the EU and UK have implemented SMA; the US agencies have signaled support for Basel’s new approach but have not finalized it.
</Rule>
