Organizations identify CDEs based on risk and business significance. For example, any data field that feeds risk-weight calculations or capital models would be a CDE. Under BCBS 239, regulators effectively treat all material risk data as CDEs. Good data governance frameworks inventory CDEs and assign them owners and stewards. The owner (often a senior business manager) is responsible for the definition and quality of each CDE; stewards implement controls and fixes.

Marking data as “critical” means prioritizing quality rules, monitoring, and issue management for those fields. The idea is to avoid the opposite of the CDE philosophy: that all data gets equal, impractical scrutiny. By focusing on CDEs, banks can allocate resources to where mistakes matter most. Identifying CDEs is not a one-time task; it must be revisited as regulations and products change.

### Permanent rule

Banks must explicitly identify and govern their critical data elements (CDEs) to ensure governance efforts focus on high-impact fields.

### Permanent trap

Treating all data elements alike; without highlighting CDEs, governance becomes spread too thin.

### Never confuse

**CDE** — A specific field deemed critical (e.g. a risk exposure amount).
---
**Data catalog** — A comprehensive inventory of all data assets (it may list CDEs but also all other fields).

### Jurisdiction variants

**No delta.** The notion of identifying and governing CDEs is best practice internationally (no one sets different rules on which fields count as “critical”).
