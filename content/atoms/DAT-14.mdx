For example, a completeness rule might state that 99% of trade records must have an associated counterparty ID. If only 97% do, that misses the threshold and triggers an exception report. Exception management then enters: the issue is logged with severity, root causes are analyzed (e.g. missing entries in a source feed), and corrective action is taken (perhaps a process fix or data backfill). Exception tracking tools or ticket systems typically record these issues.

Banks document quality rules in their governance frameworks, often aligning with BCBS 239’s accuracy/integrity principle. At each reporting cycle, automated validators run the rules. Validations can be cross-checks (e.g. sum of sub-portfolios equals total) or plausibility checks (e.g. a ratio must lie in [0,100]). When thresholds are narrowly missed, some banks issue warnings; larger gaps may halt report submission until fixed.

The **permanent expectation** is that no data anomaly goes unchecked. Adopting robust exception management means assigning issues to data stewards or owners, tracking SLA for fixes, and reporting status to management. Regulators have become wary of banks “papering over” persistent exceptions. Best practice is to classify and resolve high-impact exceptions immediately, while monitoring low-impact ones until clearance.

### Permanent rule

Every failed quality check must be logged and owned; unresolved exceptions compromise report integrity.

### Permanent trap

Archiving known data issues without resolution, assuming they are “small” or won’t recur.

### Never confuse

**Data quality rule** — A specific constraint (e.g. a value range).
---
**Threshold** — The allowable error rate (e.g. 1% missing data).

### Jurisdiction variants

**No delta.** These practices are part of robust data governance everywhere; regulators everywhere expect strict validation and issue tracking.
