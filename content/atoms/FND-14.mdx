For dated cash flows \(CF_t\) and a consistent discount rate \(r\), a simple NPV is:

\[
NPV=\sum_t \frac&#123;CF_t&#125;&#123;(1+r)^t&#125;
\]

Inflows are usually represented with one sign and outflows with the opposite sign. Discounting prevents a loss or benefit occurring far in the future from being treated as economically identical to the same nominal amount occurring immediately.

Consider an expected shortfall of 100 one year from today and a 10% annual discount rate. Its present value is \(100/1.10=90.91\). If the same 100 shortfall occurred two years away, its present value would be \(100/1.10^2=82.64\). The nominal loss is unchanged; its valuation date is not.

Expected-credit-loss accounting adds another layer: **uncertainty**. Possible credit shortfalls can be probability-weighted, while discounting separately reflects the timing of those shortfalls. IFRS 9 explicitly requires expected credit losses to be discounted to the reporting date using the effective interest rate determined at initial recognition, or an approximation, subject to specified rules for particular instruments.

That IFRS requirement must not be exported mechanically to every jurisdiction. US CECL does not prescribe one mandatory estimation technique; discounted cash-flow methods are available, but other compliant methods can be used. Indian scheduled commercial banks remain under RBI prudential provisioning rather than Ind AS 109.

The permanent mathematical lesson is that timing matters. The regulatory or accounting question is **whether, how and at what rate the applicable framework requires that timing to enter the loss measure**.

<Rule label="PERMANENT RULE">
NPV compares dated amounts only after translating them to a common valuation date.
</Rule>

<Trap label="PERMANENT TRAP">
Applying IFRS 9's EIR discounting rule to an Indian scheduled commercial bank or assuming US CECL requires a discounted-cash-flow method in every case.
</Trap>

<NeverConfuse a="Expected cash shortfall" b="Present value of a cash shortfall">
**Expected cash shortfall** — the relevant difference in cash flows under a loss methodology.
---
**Present value of a cash shortfall** — that shortfall after adjusting for its timing using the applicable discount rule.
</NeverConfuse>

<Delta j="EU">

</Delta>

<Recall
  q="Why does the date of an expected loss matter?"
  a="Because, where discounting applies, losses at different dates have different present values."
