For binary outcome *Y* (default=1, non-default=0), logistic regression assumes:
```
logit(p) = ln(p/(1-p)) = β₀ + β₁x₁ + ... + βₖxₖ.
```
Here p = P(default|x). Solving gives p = exp(linear)/(1+exp(linear)). This keeps p between 0 and 1. In credit scoring, predictors x are often applicant attributes (income, delinquencies, etc.).

**Interpretation:** β_j is the change in log-odds per unit increase in x_j. Exponentiating gives the *odds ratio* (OR): exp(β_j) is how many times the odds multiply when x_j increases by 1. For example, if β_income = -0.2, then OR=exp(-0.2)=0.82, meaning each extra $10k of income (if x_j scaled per 10k) multiplies odds of default by 0.82 (18% reduction). In scorecards, such coefficients are often translated to points.

Logistic regression doesn’t assume normal residuals, but assumes linear logit and independent observations. It handles multicollinearity same as linear (high VIF is a problem). Sample size should be adequate: rule of thumb ~10 events per predictor.

**Why it’s workhorse:** Because the output is a probability, intuitively meaningful. Regulators and credit officers can interpret “Probability of Default” directly. Also, logistic outputs are monotonic and unbounded in either direction (unlike linear which could predict &lt;0 or &gt;1). Many scorecards use logistic or simplified versions of it.

**Industry bins &amp; WOE:** Often continuous predictors are binned and converted to Weight-of-Evidence (WOE) to ensure monotonic relationships. WOE transforms category’s default rates into log-odds, aligning with logistic assumptions. Then a logistic on WOE-encoded features is linear in log-odds, making the model easier to interpret in PD terms.

### Permanent rule

Always express logistic coefficients in odds ratios for interpretation: OR = exp(β). For example, OR&lt;1 means higher x reduces default odds; OR&gt;1 means increases. Double-check that no predicted p&lt;0 or &gt;1 (should never happen in logistic).

### Permanent trap

Misreading coefficients: thinking β itself is the percentage point change in probability. It is not. Only the odds (exp(β)) have the multiplicative effect. E.g. β= -0.1 doesn’t mean 10% drop in PD; it means ~10% drop in odds (OR≈0.90).

### Never confuse

**Odds** — ratio p/(1-p) of default vs non-default probability.
---
**Probability** — p itself (0 to 1). Logistic uses odds in its link. People often mistakenly add β to p directly; the linearity is in log-odds, not in probability.

### Jurisdiction variants

**No delta.** Logistic regression is standard in all jurisdictions for credit scoring. Some regulators (e.g. RBI) allow logistic scorecards for retail IRB models, given interpretability needs. All use the same math.
