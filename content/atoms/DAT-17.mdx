In a centralized model, a bank-wide data council or steering committee makes decisions on data standards and conflicts. This ensures consistency but can be slow to react. In a federated model, each line of business governs its own data (loans, payments, etc.) with a chief data steward, under high-level corporate policy. This is more agile but risks inconsistent rules. Many banks use a hybrid: enterprise-wide standards set by a central group, with subcommittees or local owners refining for their area.

Choosing a model depends on bank size and culture. Large global banks often need a strong central committee (reporting to the board) to coordinate regional offices. A smaller bank might use a subject-based structure (e.g. separate governance for retail vs corporate banking data). RBI’s guidance suggests ownership should follow business functions, with an executive data governance committee and clear roles.

Regardless of model, a permanent rule is that the structure and responsibilities must be documented. A trap is letting data governance stay informal or ad hoc. Best practice: publish an operating model (committees, roles, processes), and review it periodically. The goal is a sustainable model where policies are enforced, quality is measured (e.g. via KPIs), and issue remediation is managed end-to-end.

         Corporate Data Governance (policy framework)

### Permanent rule

Define and publish your data governance structure (committees, roles, processes) and measure its effectiveness.

### Permanent trap

Believing “one size fits all” – e.g. centralizing without business input can lead to resistance. Balance central oversight with local expertise.

### Never confuse

**Centralized governance** — A single corporate body governs all data decisions.

---

**Federated governance** — Each division/business unit has autonomy (within overall guidelines).

### Jurisdiction variants

**No delta.** These are organizational choices; regulators expect some governance model, but don’t mandate a specific structure.
