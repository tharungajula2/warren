Wrong-way risk (WWR) means exposure and credit risk are positively correlated. If exposure to a bank is higher exactly when that bank is more likely to default, losses can be severe. Specific WWR examples include: a bank sells credit protection on itself, or an airline enters a fuel swap where fuel price spikes hurt the airline’s credit. General WWR examples include: a corporate bond dealer holds interest rate swaps with companies; if interest rates rise, swap exposure grows while those companies’ creditworthiness may fall.

Banks must identify both general and specific WWR. Specific WWR often comes from linked transactions or collateral (e.g. dealer pledges defaultable collateral). General WWR emerges from broad market shifts (e.g. recession). Basel rules and supervisory guidance require monitoring of WWR through stress tests. According to Fed guidance: “Wrong-way risk occurs when the exposure to a counterparty is positively correlated with the probability of default of the counterparty itself. Specific WWR arises from the nature of the transactions; general WWR arises from market factors”.

<Rule label="PERMANENT RULE">
Banks must identify WWR and avoid taking on transactions where exposure and counterparty strength move together. WWR should be quantified in stress scenarios.
</Rule>

<Trap label="PERMANENT TRAP">
Ignoring correlations between market moves and credit risk. For example, assuming collateral fully protects without considering if collateral value is itself at risk in the same scenario.
</Trap>

<NeverConfuse a="General Wrong-Way Risk" b="Specific Wrong-Way Risk">
**General Wrong-Way Risk** — Exposure correlated with broad market factors affecting credit (macro-driven).
---
**Specific Wrong-Way Risk** — Exposure directly linked to counterparty’s default risk (contract-specific link).
</NeverConfuse>

<Rule label="NO DELTA">
**No delta.** WWR concepts are universal in Basel/ICS framework.
</Rule>

<Recall
  q="What is specific WWR?"
  a="When exposure to a counterparty is directly tied to that counterparty’s own condition. For example, pledging weakly rated collateral from the same counterparty can create specific WWR."
/>

<Recall
  q="What is general WWR?"
  a="Exposure rises when general market stress makes the counterparty more likely to default (e.g. a swap exposed to rates with a counterparty whose credit is rate-sensitive)."
