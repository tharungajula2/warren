Regulators set specific confidence levels and horizons for capital calculation.  Basel II used 99% VaR over 10 trading days; FRTB uses 97.5% ES over 10 days.  The longer holding period corresponds to liquidating or hedging positions under stress.  One often scales 1-day VaR to a 10-day VaR by multiplying by √10 (assuming independent returns), but if volatility changes or liquidity constraints exist, that simple scaling breaks down.  

Under a normal return assumption, 10-day 99% VaR numerically equals 10-day 97.5% ES (both ≈2.33σ).  Basel chose 97.5% ES in part to align capital ratios (since ES at 97.5% on a normal distribution roughly matches 99% VaR).  Confidence level governs the tail probability (e.g. 99% VaR ignores top 1% losses).  Basel also introduced a separate stressed calibration (see MRK-10) using a 250-day period.

<Rule label="PERMANENT RULE">
Capital VaR/ES increases with higher confidence and longer horizon. Under i.i.d. assumptions, risk approximately scales with the square root of time.
</Rule>

<Trap label="PERMANENT TRAP">
Blindly scaling VaR by √time when returns are autocorrelated or volatility shifts; treating 99% VaR as equivalent to 97.5% ES without regard to distribution.
</Trap>

<NeverConfuse>
**1-day VaR** vs **10-day VaR** (horizon difference); **99% VaR** vs **97.5% ES** (confidence difference). For a normal distribution, 99% VaR ≈ 97.5% ES, but this breaks in crisis.
</NeverConfuse>

<Rule label="NO DELTA">
**No delta.** The concepts of confidence level and time horizon scaling are general; only the numeric standards (e.g. 97.5% ES vs 99% VaR) vary by regulation.
</Rule>

<Recall
  q="How does VaR scale if returns are independent?"
  a="Approximately by √time (e.g., 10-day VaR = √10 × 1-day VaR)."
