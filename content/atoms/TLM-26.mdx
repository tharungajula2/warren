Imagine a bank with long-term assets worth 110 in an orderly valuation and liabilities of 100. Economically it has positive net worth of 10. If 30 of liabilities must be repaid today but the bank has only 10 of immediate cash, it needs to raise another 20.

If markets will lend against the assets, or the assets can be sold near their orderly value, the timing mismatch can be bridged. If funding markets close and buyers demand a severe fire-sale discount, the bank may be unable to raise 20 without crystallising large losses. A liquidity problem has now become a solvency problem.

Banks are particularly exposed because maturity transformation is central to intermediation: shorter or withdrawable funding supports assets that often return cash over longer horizons. Basel’s liquidity principles explicitly recognise this structural vulnerability.

Capital does not mechanically solve the timing problem. Equity absorbs losses and protects creditors from asset-value deterioration, but it is not a pile of settlement cash. A well-capitalised bank still needs immediately usable liquidity, collateral and access to payment systems. Conversely, holding liquidity does not cure true negative net worth: cash can postpone but not erase losses exceeding capital.

This distinction explains why bank resilience requires both capital and liquidity frameworks. Capital absorbs unexpected losses; liquidity buffers and funding arrangements bridge cash-flow timing under stress. Treasury and ALM must therefore ask both questions whenever stress appears: **what is the value loss?** and **when is cash needed?**

### Permanent rule

Positive net worth cannot substitute for cash at the moment an obligation must settle.

### Permanent trap

Answering a liquidity challenge by pointing to a strong capital ratio without demonstrating how the next cash obligation will actually be paid.

### Never confuse

**Solvency** — adequacy of asset value and loss-absorbing resources relative to liabilities.
---
**Liquidity** — ability to obtain cash in time to meet obligations without unacceptable loss.

### Jurisdiction variants

**No delta.** The distinction follows balance-sheet economics, not jurisdiction.
