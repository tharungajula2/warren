Money received now and the same nominal amount received later are not economically identical when a positive required return exists. Current money can be invested, while a future payment cannot be used until it arrives. **Present value (PV)** expresses a future cash flow in today's value using a **discount rate**.

For a cash flow \(CF_t\) received \(t\) annual periods from now at a constant annually compounded rate \(r\):

\[
PV=\frac&#123;CF_t&#125;&#123;(1+r)^t&#125;
\]

The term

\[
DF_t=\frac&#123;1&#125;&#123;(1+r)^t&#125;
\]

is the **discount factor**. Thus \(PV=CF_t\times DF_t\).

If 110 is certain to arrive one year from now and the relevant annual discount rate is 10%, its present value is \(110/1.10=100\). At the same rate, 110 due two years away is worth less today because it is discounted for two periods.

Discounting is not synonymous with predicting whether payment occurs. A cash flow can first be probability-weighted for uncertainty and then discounted for timing. Probability changes the expected amount; discounting changes the value assigned to its timing. That distinction becomes essential in expected-loss calculations.

The rate and cash-flow convention must match. Annual cash flows require a rate expressed consistently with annual periods. Monthly timing requires a compatible periodic or appropriately converted rate. Cash flows at the start versus end of a period also produce different results. A perfectly correct formula with mismatched timing assumptions still gives the wrong answer.

Present value is mathematics rather than a banking-specific rule. Accounting standards, valuation frameworks and risk models decide **which** cash flows and **which** rate to use; the discounting operation itself does not change by jurisdiction.

```text
Future cash flow CF_t
          |
          v
+----------------------+
| Discount factor      |
| 1 / (1+r)^t          |
+----------------------+
          |
          v
+----------------------+
| Present value        |
| CF_t x discount      |
| factor               |
+----------------------+
```

### Permanent rule

Cash flows at different dates cannot be compared economically until timing has been put on a common basis.

### Permanent trap

Discounting a monthly cash flow with an annual rate as though one month were one annual period.

### Never confuse

**Discount rate** — the rate used to translate value across time.

---

**Discount factor** — the numerical multiplier produced from the rate and timing; the rate is an input, while the factor performs the multiplication.

### Jurisdiction variants

**No delta.** Present-value mathematics does not change across jurisdictions.

<Recall
  q="What is the one-period discount factor at rate \(r\)?"
  a="\(1/(1+r)\)."
/>

<Recall
  q="Does discounting by itself model default probability?"
  a="No; discounting addresses timing, while probability addresses uncertainty."
