A credit default swap index references a standardised basket of corporate or sovereign credits. Major index families include CDX and iTraxx. Instead of negotiating separate CDS trades on every constituent, a market participant can buy or sell protection on the index and obtain diversified credit-spread exposure in one contract.

The index has a series and version. Periodically, a new series is launched with a refreshed constituent set according to index rules. The old series continues to trade “off the run”. This creates an important data requirement: “five-year index” is incomplete without family, series, version and maturity.

Index premium is commonly quoted as a spread, though some higher-risk indices can trade with upfront amounts plus a standard coupon. When a constituent experiences a credit event, the index notional and cash flows are adjusted under the relevant rules. The remaining index continues to exist rather than being recreated from scratch for every default.

Credit indices serve several purposes: macro hedging of a credit portfolio, directional views on market credit spreads, relative-value trades and liquidity management. They are usually more liquid than a large set of equivalent single-name CDS positions, but basis risk arises because an institution’s actual portfolio will not exactly match index constituents, weights, maturities or seniorities.

An index is not the same as a tranche. The unfunded index provides proportional exposure to the underlying names. A tranche concentrates losses into a defined attachment/detachment layer and introduces strong non-linearity and correlation sensitivity.

For risk data, map the index contract to its legal identifier, series, constituent set and effective dates. A position can appear unchanged at the front-office label level while its risk mapping changes when a new index series becomes the market benchmark.
