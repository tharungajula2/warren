For example, a bank might observe that customers with many cellphone lines have higher default rates. This is a correlation. But is having multiple lines causing defaults? Likely not. A confounder like income or employment stability might cause both more phone lines and more default. If we ignorantly treat the phone lines variable as causal, we could erroneously deny credit to people with many phones, which is a mistake.

Statistics: we often measure correlation by Pearson’s r (for continuous), or contingency tables for categorical. But high correlation doesn’t prove one causes the other. Another example: when credit scores rose during a boom, did positive sentiment cause better credit history, or did third factors (like unemployment falling) cause both? Without random experimentation, we cannot be sure.

**Confounding** is common in observational credit data. Suppose a score model finds married customers default less. Marriage isn’t causing lower defaults per se; perhaps married people are older with steadier jobs (confounders). To claim causation, one needs to control for confounders or use techniques like propensity scoring.

Practically, risk analysts should: ask if a predictive factor makes economic sense as a cause, and check if unobserved factors could be driving both. If unsure, treat correlated predictors with caution. For example, IGNORED: days-with-current-employer might correlate with income; controlling for both avoids blaming employer tenure incorrectly.

```text
 In actual data, observation shows:
   YearsEmployed ↑  -&gt;  DefaultRate ↓
   But: YearsEmployed and Income are correlated (r=0.6).
 Confounder: Income
```

### Permanent rule

Always question causality claims in credit models. Use domain knowledge and control for confounders to avoid spurious attributions. No matter how strong a correlation, it may be caused by hidden variables.

### Permanent trap

Automatically assuming that a correlated predictor is causal. E.g., “Since region correlates with default, region causes default.” In reality, region may proxy economic conditions (the real cause). Mis-specifying this can lead to misdirected interventions.

### Never confuse

**Correlation** — statistical association between two variables’ values (e.g. high debt ratio and defaults).

---

**Causation** — one variable directly influences the other.
They are not the same: correlation is necessary but not sufficient for causation. Always consider other variables that might explain the link.

### Jurisdiction variants

**No delta.** The principle that correlation is not causation is fundamental to statistics. However, regulatory scrutiny may vary: e.g. fair lending laws (in US/EU) demand checking that models do not inadvertently use proxies for protected attributes (a form of confounding fairness).
