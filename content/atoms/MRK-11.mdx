Before the crisis, banks often relied on VaR-based models and porous TB/BB boundaries.  During 2007–08, widespread trading losses (e.g. on structured credit) showed models underestimated risks.  For instance, losses from Lehman and AIG’s trading books were far worse than their VaR predicted.  This led to Basel 2.5 (2009) which increased capital on trading products (higher charges on securitisations, addition of stressed VaR).

FRTB (Basel 3.1) was developed to fix the “major fault lines” in the old framework.  It enforces stricter TB/BB boundary (preventing regulatory arbitrage), uses ES instead of VaR (better tail capture), and adds charges for default risk (DRC) and unmodellable risks (NMRFs).  These changes aim to ensure capital better reflects true market risk.

<Rule label="PERMANENT RULE">
Capital frameworks must account for severe but plausible market moves (stress scenarios) and not rely solely on benign historical periods.
</Rule>

<Trap label="PERMANENT TRAP">
Relying only on high-confidence VaR and ignoring stress episodes.
</Trap>

<NeverConfuse>
**Basel II.5** — interim 2009 amendments (added stressed VaR and CVA capital) vs **Basel 3.1 (FRTB)** — comprehensive 2019 reforms with ES, risk sensitivities, etc.
</NeverConfuse>

<Rule label="NO DELTA">
**No delta.** All major jurisdictions have recognized the pre-crisis framework’s shortcomings.
</Rule>

<Recall
  q="What key weakness in pre-crisis market risk did FRTB address?"
  a="The underestimation of tail risk and poor boundary definitions, leading to large losses not captured by old VaR models."
/>

<Recall
