Fraud and money laundering intersect but are distinct. Fraud is illegally obtaining funds (e.g. a customer lying on a loan form, identity theft, credit card scams). Money laundering is hiding the origin of criminal funds, which can include fraud proceeds. For example, funds stolen by fraud may need laundering through the banking system. 

**First-party fraud** is when the actual account holder deceives the bank (e.g. a borrower deliberately omits liabilities on a loan application).  **Third-party fraud** (identity theft) is when someone else uses a customer’s identity or card without permission. These often trigger AML controls because stolen funds often pass through multiple accounts (see mule accounts below). 

**Application fraud** and **synthetic identity fraud** occur at onboarding: criminals submit fake or stolen IDs to open accounts. These then can be used to commit further fraud or laundering. **Mule accounts** are legitimate-looking accounts controlled by criminals. A mule (often an unwitting person) funnels stolen/fraudulent funds on behalf of others, thus blending fraud and money laundering. 

Given these overlaps, many banks now integrate Fraud and AML teams (“FRAML”). They share data and alerts, since unusual patterns might indicate either fraud or laundering. For instance, many small deposits followed by a large withdrawal could be either structured cash deposits (ML) or a fraudulent funding scheme. Combining teams improves efficiency and leverages common analytics (customer risk scoring, etc.).

<Rule label="PERMANENT RULE">
Treat fraud risk similarly to AML risk in high-end banking. Verify identities at application (to catch synthetic IDs) and monitor for typical fraud indicators (stolen card usage, unusual loan applications). Set up communication channels between fraud and AML units for suspicious cases.
</Rule>

<Trap label="PERMANENT TRAP">
Assuming fraud is only the customer’s problem. In fact, fraud can generate illicit proceeds that must be reported if laundered. Also, ignoring small-value fraud patterns because they aren’t “money laundering” – these can aggregate to significant AML issues.
</Trap>

<NeverConfuse a="First-party fraud" b="Third-party fraud (Identity theft)">
**First-party fraud** — committed by the genuine account holder (e.g. lying on an application).  
---  
**Third-party fraud (Identity theft)** — committed by someone else using stolen or synthetic credentials. These are often mixed up because both involve deception; the key is who controls the account.
</NeverConfuse>

<Rule label="NO DELTA">
**No delta.** The definitions of fraud vs AML are consistent globally. However, specifics differ: India’s RBI KYC rules expressly cover application fraud; UK’s JMLSG includes guidance on fraud as an AML risk. The requirement to report fraud or co-operate with law enforcement is universal, even though definitions of fraud types derive from general criminal law, not AML regulation.
</Rule>
