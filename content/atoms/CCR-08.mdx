Many counterparties allow rehypothecation of collateral (especially cash), effectively giving the collateral-receiver a lien on it. This can improve liquidity for dealers but introduces systemic risk. If the client counterparty defaults but its posted collateral is rehypothecated and then lost or seized, the client cannot reclaim it. As a result, regulators have restricted rehypothecation in some cases (e.g. segregated accounts), and banking regulations may require some client collateral be held in special segregated accounts.

Rehypothecation issues arose in past crises (e.g. 2008 Bear Stearns). Current guidelines encourage limiting rehypothecation or requiring full disclosure to clients. In CCR terms, rehypothecated collateral may not provide full protection: banks should consider the availability of collateral in stress (if collateral is encumbered elsewhere).

### Permanent rule

Do not double-count rehypothecated collateral. Once collateral is rehypothecated, the receiving party’s obligation to return it remains, but the collateral may not be free to use for own risk.

### Permanent trap

Assuming posted collateral is safe and available even if rehypothecated. In reality, if the receiving bank is insolvent, the collateral may be tied up.

### Never confuse

**Pledged Collateral** — Collateral a counterparty provides as security.
---
**Rehypothecation** — The reuse of pledged collateral by the receiver. These are not the same; rehypothecated collateral may be co-mingled with others’ assets.

### Jurisdiction variants

**No delta.** Rules on rehypothecation may vary legally, but concept is global. Many jurisdictions allow limited rehypothecation under CSAs.
